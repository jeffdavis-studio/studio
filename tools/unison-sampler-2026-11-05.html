Unison — sampler keyboard

The studio's Fairlight and Emulator. Its disk holds ten one-shots, each rendered live in your browser from the band's own synths (the Juno, the OB-Xa, the FM and the Minimoog, plus the vocoder's vowel bank for the choir), then burned to an 8-bit chip and played back through the drum machine's converter. Nothing here is a recording. Each sound was made at one note, and the whole keyboard plays from it by changing the playback clock, so it gets brighter and grainier going up and darker and longer going down, the way the 1983 machines did.

Rendering the disk…

Eight voices: a ninth note cuts the oldest. The keys cover the sound's playable range, and the marked key is the note it was made at. Held sounds loop. The choir, the strings, the texture and the bass hold for as long as the key is down by repeating a short stretch of the sample (a whole number of cycles, cut at a zero crossing), then fade on release. Hits play out. The orchestra hit, the horn stab and the mallet ignore how long you hold the key, and their tail is the chip running out. The breath stops when you let go.

The same dial as the Linn's. Left is crunchy, short 8-bit chips (the early drum machines). Right is cleaner, longer 12-bit chips (the 1984 machines). The sampler opens a little left of the LinnDrum, so the keyboard sounds slightly grainier than the drums beside it. Moving the knob re-burns the whole disk.

How the disk is made and what was measured

Level. Each sound is matched, within 1 dB at its root and at both ends of its range, to the synth patch it replaces on the same channel strip. A key-scaling slope on each sound does the matching across the range.

Different from the synth. Each sound was measured against the patch it replaces: brightness, attack shape, tail, top end and grain. It had to differ on at least two of the five. Single dark patches burned on their own failed that test, so most entries are layers that no one synth plays.
